Finders Keepers in 1493 and Now

Published: October 4, 2026 • 9 min read

Section: Field Notes & Perspectives • Digital Infrastructure, Law & Economics

In May of 1493, Pope Alexander VI sat in Rome with a quill and a fresh map of half an ocean.

Christopher Columbus had only just returned to the court of Ferdinand and Isabella, bearing tales of Caribbean islands, parrot feathers, and unbaptized shores. Fearing that Portuguese caravels would round the southern horn of Africa and claim the western seas, the Spanish Crown petitioned the Borgia Pope for title. Alexander drew an imaginary meridian from pole to pole, one hundred leagues west of the Azores and Cape Verde. Everything discovered to the west and south was granted in perpetuity to the Catholic monarchs of Spain; everything to the east was preserved for the Crown of Portugal.

King John II of Portugal looked at the papal chart and saw an ocean pinched shut. With only one hundred leagues west of the islands, Portuguese fleets tacking into the South Atlantic would have no sea room to swing wide around storms, and his navigators suspected land lay further southwest. He refused the decree. The following summer, at a small Castilian town called Tordesillas, diplomats from Lisbon and Madrid sat down without the Pope to redraw the meridian: three hundred and seventy leagues west of Cape Verde.

When Pedro Álvares Cabral swung his ships wide into the Atlantic in 1500, he caught the eastern hump of South America. That line gave Portugal what is now Brazil. Thirty-five years later, after Magellan’s survivors sailed into the Moluccas from the east, the two crowns sat down once more at Zaragoza, drew an anti-meridian through the Pacific, and closed the circle. The globe was partitioned between two Iberian treasuries by virtue of ink, latitude, and papal bull.

Hearing of the division, King Francis I of France made no formal diplomatic protest. He simply asked his courtiers:

“Show me the clause in Father Adam’s will that divides the earth between my brothers of Spain and Portugal and excludes me from my share.”

A quiet administrative notification landed in registrar mailboxes this morning:

"We're writing to let you know that the registry behind .com will be increasing the regular wholesale price, starting on November 1, 2026 04:00 UTC. This change affects each and every registrar that offers it... Registrations can be stacked up to 10 years in advance."

On an individual domain invoice, the shift from $11.08 to $11.81 is financially meaningless. Nobody’s balance sheet breaks over seventy-three cents.

Yet treating this notice as a trivial price adjustment misses the structural reality behind it. When every competitive registrar on the planet—Porkbun, Cloudflare, Namecheap, GoDaddy—is forced to raise its baseline at the exact same second, this ceases to be a retail event.

It is a legal and regulatory artifact: a ten-year-old story in the "not making," where administrative inertia, contractual monopolies, and rent-seeking quietly masquerade as standard utility inflation.

1. The Separation of Law and Justice at the Root

In the architecture of legal institutions, there is a fundamental dividing line: The law determines what is permitted to count; justice asks what is actually there.

The root of the internet was born out of public research grants, academic labs, and open collaborative protocols. Nobody invented .com as a private corporate toll road. Yet through decades of administrative delegation, the technical registry for over 160 million active domains was consolidated into an exclusive, perpetual concession operated by Verisign, Inc. (VRSN).

From a purely legal perspective, Verisign’s pricing power is spotless. It operates under a formal Cooperative Agreement with the National Telecommunications and Information Administration (NTIA, U.S. Department of Commerce) and a corresponding Registry Agreement with ICANN.

From an economic perspective, however, the arrangement inverts every principle of competitive markets:

  • Operating Margins Exceeding 65%–70%: In modern cloud engineering, the marginal cost to store and resolve a DNS record has collapsed over the past twenty-five years. Public filings and industry cost analyses demonstrate that maintaining an authoritative TLD registry costs roughly $2.50 to $3.00 per domain-year. Verisign charges a wholesale fee of $10.26, jumping to $10.97 on November 1.
  • Capital Return vs. Innovation: Because the registry requires no research breakthroughs, consumer product cycles, or competitive marketing, Verisign functions primarily as a financial engineering vehicle. Nearly every dollar of free cash flow—roughly $800M+ annually—is channeled directly into share buybacks, inflating EPS while its balance sheet operates in a negative stockholder equity position.

The law protects the concession as an authorized contract. Justice observes an irreplaceable public namespace taxed to generate guaranteed private rent.

2. The 7% Escalator: The Illusion of Public Oversight

The current escalation pattern is neither accidental nor sudden. It is a predictable institutional cycle:

  • In 2012, the U.S. Department of Commerce stepped in to freeze wholesale .com pricing at $7.85, explicitly concluding that market competition did not exist in the domain registry space and that price caps were essential to protect the public from monopoly exploitation.
  • In 2018, under an amended Cooperative Agreement, that protection was quietly removed. The revised terms granted Verisign the right to increase wholesale prices by up to 7% per year in the final four years of each six-year agreement cycle, with zero requirement to demonstrate an increase in underlying hardware, security, or operating costs.
  • 2024 through 2026 represents the activation of that exact contractual clause.

The irony of administrative oversight is that during public comment windows, over 90% of submitted public and industry comments routinely oppose these automatic escalations. Yet the regulatory machinery defaults to preservation: taking the contract out to competitive open bidding carries perceived administrative risk, while rubber-stamping the "presumptive right of renewal" maintains the status quo.

3. The Myth of the Jurisdictional Standoff

Discussions surrounding global internet governance frequently invoke a supposed geopolitical clash: Why does an American corporation control a global namespace? Why haven’t European or international antitrust watchdogs dismantled the tollbooth?

The assumption that European and American regulators stand on opposite sides of this issue is a misconception. Behind the diplomatic language, the actual institutional participants across Washington, London, and Brussels operate on a largely shared understanding:

  1. Parallel Antitrust Philosophy: Regulators in both the U.S. (DOJ Antitrust, FTC) and the EU (DG COMP) broadly recognize that natural digital monopolies should reflect declining underlying compute and network transit costs.
  2. Administrative Paralysis: The barrier to reform is not an ideological fight between nations; it is the sheer weight of bureaucratic friction. Because the Registry Agreement is structured as a multi-party pact bound to U.S. choice-of-law and arbitral forums, foreign regulators have historically avoided extraterritorial enforcement actions that would spark an unnecessary diplomatic impasse over root-zone mechanics.
  3. The Multi-Stakeholder Incentive Trap: ICANN itself collects transactional registry fees from every .com registration. When registry revenues remain stable and legally protected, the institutional apparatus tasked with overseeing the global DNS is itself financially insulated.

Everyone in the room understands the economic distortion; nobody inside the administrative room has an incentive to dismantle it.

4. The Shift to the Courts: Kessler v. VeriSign (September 2026)

Because administrative agencies and international bodies have spent ten years allowing the agreement to coast on autopilot, the issue has finally been forced into the judicial branch.

On September 4, 2026, an antitrust class action was formally filed in the U.S. District Court for the Central District of California: Kessler v. VeriSign Inc. and ICANN (Case No. 2:26-cv-09960).

The complaint attacks the heart of the regulatory compromise under Sections 1 and 2 of the Sherman Act, alleging that:

  • ICANN and Verisign entered into an unlawful pricing conspiracy, replacing independent multi-stakeholder governance with a revenue-sharing pact.
  • Billions of dollars in artificial overcharges were systematically extracted from millions of domain owners under the cover of administrative silence.

Verisign will inevitably invoke the state-action immunity doctrine, arguing that because the Department of Commerce authorized the contract, the conduct is immune from private antitrust liability. Whether the courts accept that defense or rule that administrative oversight had lapsed into passive rubber-stamping will determine whether the .com toll road can ever be challenged under domestic law.

5. Ten Years in the "Not Making"

The registrar’s parting recommendation—to stack renewals up to ten years in advance to lock in current rates—is technically practical advice. But it serves as an indictment of the system itself.

When the primary mechanism available to businesses and builders to protect against monopoly pricing is to prepay a decade of rent to the toll collector, the market has not solved the problem. It has merely adapted to the distortion.

The .com pricing question was never about seventy-three cents. It is a ten-year-old case study in how public commons become private concessions, how contracts replace competition, and how institutions designed to protect the public end up protecting the tollbooth instead.
